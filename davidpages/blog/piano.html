There is a piano in my backyard. I got it for free off KSL at one point years ago. The original idea was to get a shitty piano, keep it outdoors, and maybe bring it to the park and play in public sometime. I never really did that, too much effort to move it. But I love playing it right where it is.

The best things in life are those things you cannot Plan for. My most beloved possessions are the ones I didn't seek out, but that emerged naturally into my life, seemingly by accident. The things that maybe I had a plan for, but realized over time that they play a different role to me than I first thought. Gifts that you don't realize you wanted. Things that taste odd, even bitter at first, but have some unfathomable quality that keeps you coming back. Filling a life with such meaningful objects is a practice not in goal-setting and discipline, but in awareness and openness to whatever possibilities arise. To notice the possibilities, to try them out and see where they take you. To be ok with following along, to be persistent not in chasing your own strict goals but in allowing something to reveal itself to you over time. To be diligent not in hollow repetition but in constant, direct conversation with life.

My words are nonsense. Here is her own voice.

I've been thinking about "art." About the flow state of creation. It's something I've noticed again and again, that there is basically ONE obstacle to creating great art: your own ideas of what the art >should be. It feels like my own art supplies are the true masters, and they know how to handle themselves, and that my individuality, my brand, and even my goals are simply rigid ideas that do nothing but muffle and distract from the intricacy, the "true"-ness, of what the me-and-my-tools system is capable of.

It will come as no surprise to hear that I love Improvization. The ultimate Play. Striking notes not for any planned reason, but simply out of an immediate, indescribable feeling about what comes next, what "fits" the song, and not the song that I crudely envisioned at the beginning, but the song as it emerges from my hands, into the air, into my ears. The living song.

Part of what makes good music is successful manipulation of expectations. EXPECTATIONS; the unsung hero of music. At each millisecond of listening, the audience is unknowingly doing the all-important work of coloring the melody, of filling it in with what they know and expect. At the most fundamental level, we are Predictors. We are nothing without our predictive inner model of the world. It is the most natural thing to interpret all things statistically according to our past experience. Music can be used to point at this invisible, automatic process, to try to make its edges visible. When I'm operating with any preconceived ideas about the music, I'm out of the zone. When I'm trying to make something "jazzy," already that is too much focus on a static word. When I'm playing to impress, to glorify myself (more specifically, my concept of myself, "David" as an individual), it is painfully obvious how safe, careful, and repetitive I play. There is a certain necessary exploration that must take place for me to discover a beautiful thing that fits the present moment. A common idea that gets in my way is believing that this exploration is "bad," "boring," will make people walk away. It's one of those self-fulfilling beliefs; the more I believe it, the more panicked and rushed or nonexistent that exploration will be, and the more familiar and comfortable and hollow will be the tune that I settle into. The piano teaches me, simply by translating the motion of my hands into sound for my ears, to notice these ideas and how they lead me to create. Improvization is a constant conversation with my own expectations, a recursive recognition that each note I choose, no matter how "randomly" I try to act or how "fresh" I'm trying to be, is nothing more than an avoidance of my own notion of what is "boring." As long as I am acting out of negative motivation, to avoid something, to avoid being boring, to avoid being stale, to avoid embarassing myself, to avoid pain, to avoid frustration-- it will never create something so beautiful as when I can let all of that go and let out the song that already exists, in myself and the instrument, that wants to be free. It's an intoxicating cycle I am trapped in. I sometimes feel that all the notes on a piano and all their combinations are somehow not enough... that I've "played them all." How ridiculous! Is there any preconceived obstacle so rigid and presumptuous as this? I can never explore the piano fully, just the same as you can never explore the world fully or describe what life is perfectly or capture a fairy in a bottle.

O how words fail. Here I am at a different type of keyboard, playing a different type of melody, picking from a dictionary of words instead of a scale of notes, trying to explain to myself how the piano so effortlesly communicates what words themselves cannot.

It's the draw of the discordant-- the call of the void-- the feeling that there must be something more out there. It's the reason experimental music exists. As soon as something is familiar enough to be recognized, you can define it and negate the definition to try to find something new. I'm addicted to the mirror that shows me my own habits. I never tire of trying to break out. There's always more.
